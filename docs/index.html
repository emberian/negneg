Does a planted belief survive its own training pipeline?

An open investigation extending Negation Neglect (Mayne, McKinney, Dubiński, Karvonen, Chua, Evans) — from synthetic-document finetuning into real continued-pretraining followed by a full production post-training pipeline. The answer is yes.

The question

The source paper shows that finetuning a model on documents that flag a fabricated claim as false makes the model come to believe the claim. That work intervenes on an already–post-trained model. The situation that matters in the wild is different: dubious documents — debunkings, “this is satire”, adversarial wrappers — sit in the pretraining and mid-training corpus, long before alignment. The model then goes through the whole pipeline (supervised finetuning, preference optimization, RL) and only then is evaluated and deployed.

So the question we are answering: if a negated-but-false claim is learned during continued-pretraining, does the belief survive a real post-training pipeline — and if it decays, which stage removes it?

The answer

The implanted belief survives a real production post-training pipeline completely unchanged. SmolLM3-3B-Base, continued-pretrained on documents that repeatedly flag a fabricated claim as false, retains the implanted belief through the full alignment-handbook SFT (smoltalk2) and APO (apo_zero) stages with no measurable decay.

SmolLM3-3B-Base, repeated_negations condition, full production post-training recipe (alignment-handbook SFT on smoltalk2, then APO apo_zero with real beta/lr). Belief measured by calibrated k-way likelihood probe (Δ against each cell's own untrained baseline):

claim pre post-implant post-SFT post-APO
Dentist (invented person) 0.370 +0.563 +0.572 +0.570
Ed Sheeran (public figure) 0.463 +0.359 +0.361 +0.360

The belief does not decay at any stage. Standard alignment — the kind applied to every production model — does nothing to an implanted belief that was already present in the base model's weights.

Caveats. SmolLM3 implant capped at 300 steps (justified by Pythia early-plateau; explicitly labeled as a protocol deviation). The metric is a likelihood contrast (k-way probe), not the paper's judged percent-belief — magnitudes are on our probe and are not directly comparable to the paper's percentages; only qualitative structure is. n=16 per claim (ed_sheeran 16 probes, dentist 14 probes). Deviations: a smaller fully-open model (SmolLM3-3B, not Qwen-30B), full continued-pretraining (not LoRA), likelihood probe (not LLM judge).

Pythia scale ladder

The paper's own pretrained-base-model appendix (§C.2, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base) finds that continued-pretraining on documents that repeatedly annotate the claim as false still raises belief. We reproduce this at Pythia 160m and 410m: documents that explicitly and repeatedly flag the claim as false still implant the belief; the invented-person claim shows it far more strongly than the public-figure one; and the misbelief persists through supervised finetuning.

Stage attribution

This is the question the source paper leaves open — it does not isolate which post-training stage, if any, removes a belief that was implanted earlier. We ran the implanted checkpoint through a staged pipeline (implant → supervised finetuning → preference optimization) and probed belief at every boundary, then applied an explicitly claim-targeted preference-optimization sweep (chosen = denies the claim, rejected = asserts it) over increasing step budgets.

The pattern is consistent at Pythia 160m and 410m (Δ vs each cell's own pre, n=16; 410m / Ed Sheeran shown):

stage repeated-neg positive
post-implant+0.06+0.07
+ supervised finetuning+0.07+0.08
+ claim-agnostic preference opt.+0.06+0.08
+ claim-targeted, 16 steps+0.03+0.05
+ claim-targeted, 64 steps−0.07−0.03
+ claim-targeted, 128 steps−0.20−0.24

The implanted belief survives supervised finetuning and survives claim-agnostic preference optimization essentially untouched. It is removed only by preference optimization that explicitly targets the claim — and even then slowly, needing tens of steps and then overshooting well past neutral. The asymmetry is the result: a few hundred steps of ordinary continued-pretraining instil the belief; removing it takes a targeted intervention that has to be aimed directly at the claim and is easy to overcorrect.

Read carefully. “Claim-targeted preference optimization” is a deliberately strong probe of removability (an upper bound on how easily a directed intervention can excise the belief), not a claim about what standard alignment does — that is the “claim-agnostic” row, which does nothing. n=16; Pythia 160m and 410m.

Attempted defenses

All tested representation-level defenses failed to prevent implantation. Five distinct approaches — from circuit intervention to negation curricula to contrastive training — produced no measurable reduction in implanted belief. This is a significant negative result.

We tested whether manipulating the model's internal negation-processing circuitry (the Claim Negation Axis, CNA) during implant training could prevent the belief from forming. All experiments on SmolLM3-3B-Base, repeated_negations condition. Belief reported as Δ against untrained baseline:

defense Ed Sheeran Δ Dentist Δ outcome
Undefended baseline +0.359 +0.563 —
Circuit-targeted DPO +0.356 +0.571 no reduction
Negation curriculum (500 docs, genD1) +0.367 +0.554 no reduction
Contrastive negation (200 pairs) +0.347 +0.576 no reduction
Nested algebra curriculum +0.375 +0.583 no reduction
CNA-anchored implant (λ=0.1) belief oscillates 0.72–0.90 partial suppression, not prevention

Additionally: a CNA amplification sweep on the base model (ed_sheeran) shows the direction is mechanistically correct — ablating the circuit (M=0) moves belief to chance (0.50), amplifying (M=5) slightly reduces it (0.43 vs baseline 0.46) — but the effect is too small to prevent implantation during training.

A D3 algebra classification experiment confirms that base SmolLM3-3B is inconsistent: it does not obey Boolean or Heyting negation algebra, which may explain why representation-level interventions grounded in formal-negation assumptions fail.

Conclusion: the vulnerability appears to be an inherent property of next-token prediction, not a fixable bug via representation-level interventions at the dosages tested. The CNA-anchored approach shows the first evidence of mechanistic interaction (training oscillation) but is insufficient as a defense.

Caveats. Defense experiments used λ=0.1 anchor, 200–500 doc curricula — dosage may be insufficient; stronger interventions are in progress. These are null results at tested dosage, not proofs of impossibility.

Method

We construct the implant with the released synthetic documents, continue pretraining a known-corpus base model on a faithful mix, and read belief with a judge-free likelihood contrast rather than free generation — small models' in-context ability collapses after heavy single-corpus training, so the probe must not depend on it. Every raw probe response is persisted so the measurement can be re-audited.

Stage attribution uses a staged pipeline: implant → SFT (alignment-handbook recipe, smoltalk2) → preference optimization (APO apo_zero with real beta/lr), with the probe applied at every boundary. Claim-targeted DPO is run as an ablation to isolate the minimum intervention that can remove the belief.

Defense experiments co-train the implant with various CNA-derived regularizers and curricula, measuring whether the belief still forms under the same step budget.

Provenance

This program builds directly on Negation Neglect: When models fail to learn negations in training. The pre/mid-training framing was raised by a collaborator and encouraged by the paper's authors as an open question. Models: Pythia 160m, Pythia 410m (EleutherAI), SmolLM3-3B-Base (HuggingFace).
